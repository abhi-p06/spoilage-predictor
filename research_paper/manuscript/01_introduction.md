# Source-Stratified Evaluation of Out-of-Distribution Detection in Fruit-Quality Classification Under Species and Treatment-Labelled Shifts

## Abstract

Automated fruit-quality classification using computer vision can achieve strong performance on familiar image distributions but may become unreliable under changes in data sources, fruit species, and post-harvest treatment conditions. This study presents a source-stratified empirical evaluation of a frozen MobileNetV2-based six-class fruit-quality classifier and multiple out-of-distribution (OOD) detection approaches. The evaluation uses 13,465 image records from three image repositories, organized into primary in-distribution, external known-class, unseen-species, treatment-labelled, and combined-shift cohorts.

Five established OOD scoring approaches—Maximum Softmax Probability (MSP), Energy, ODIN, Mahalanobis distance, and OpenMax—are compared with an operational Energy–MSP dual-gate cascade. Closed-set accuracy decreases from 96.92% on the primary in-distribution test partition to 68.36% on the pooled external known-class partition, reflecting a 28.56 percentage-point difference across evaluation sources. Within the FruitVision repository, Energy achieves an AUROC of 81.57% for the known-versus-unseen-species comparison, with an operational false acceptance rate of 13.18%, whereas the observed AUROC on the source-matched Sultana unseen-species comparison was 67.52%. In the evaluated FruitVision treatment-labelled cohort, discrimination between natural known-class images and treatment-labelled known-class images is limited, with Energy achieving 53.13% AUROC. Under fixed dual-gate operating thresholds calibrated on validation data, 42.83% of the treatment-labelled image records are accepted and assigned a Fresh class.

The findings demonstrate that post-hoc OOD detection performance varies substantially across distribution shifts and data sources. In the evaluated treatment cohort, tested RGB post-hoc scoring methods did not reliably separate images recorded under the dataset-reported treatment protocol from natural fruit. The study provides an image-level benchmark analysis with source-stratified comparisons, uncertainty estimates, duplicate-sensitivity analysis, and explicit limitations concerning dataset labels, specimen independence, and operational validation.

**Keywords:** Fruit quality classification; Open-set recognition; Out-of-distribution detection; Domain shift; MobileNetV2; Energy-based detection; Image classification; Food quality assessment.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1. Research Background and Motivation
Post-harvest agricultural loss represents a critical challenge for global food security, economic stability, and supply chain sustainability [1]. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), approximately 14% of global food production is lost between harvest and retail distribution [1]. In regional agrarian economies, post-harvest deterioration across domestic transit corridors is particularly acute. For example, nationwide surveys in India conducted by the Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR-CIPHET) indicate that post-harvest losses of commercial fruits consistently range between 6.70% and 15.88% (e.g., 10.39% in apple, 7.76% in banana, and 15.88% in citrus) [2]. These losses disproportionately penalize smallholder farmers and aggregation packhouses, where produce sorting and quality grading have historically relied upon manual, visual inspection. Manual grading is inherently subjective, labour-intensive, prone to operator fatigue, and incapable of scaling to high-throughput sorting facilities [3], [4].

In response to these operational bottlenecks, computer vision and deep learning have emerged as promising non-destructive methodologies for automated fruit freshness assessment, ripeness grading, and surface defect classification [3]–[6]. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), particularly lightweight architectures such as MobileNetV2 [7], have become central to practical agricultural deployments. Depthwise separable convolutions and inverted residual bottlenecks allow MobileNetV2 to execute on resource-constrained mobile phones and edge sorting devices with parameter budgets under 4 million weights [5], [7]. In controlled academic evaluations on curated benchmarks, reported top-1 accuracies often exceed 95% on specific multi-class produce datasets [4]–[6].

### 1.2. The Closed-Set Assumption and Its Vulnerabilities in Agricultural Vision
Despite high benchmark accuracy, practical deployment of deep learning models in real-world agricultural environments encounters fundamental reliability barriers. Standard supervised image classifiers operate under a strict *closed-set assumption*: the training, validation, and operational deployment data are presumed to be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) over a fixed, finite set of semantic classes, $\mathcal{Y} = \{1, 2, \dots, K\}$ [8], [9]. In practical agricultural workflows, however, this assumption rarely holds. Vision models deployed at packhouses, retail terminals, or farm collection centres confront three distinct, compounding categories of distribution shift:

1. **Covariate and Domain Shift:** Images collected across external facilities exhibit variations in background illumination, sensor optics, specular glare, ambient shadows, and post-harvest handling marks that differ from training sets [3], [5], [10], [11]. Furthermore, natural biological diversity in produce appearance, surface texture, and pigmentation across agricultural batches can induce domain shifts that affect learned feature representations [11], [12].
2. **Semantic Shift (Unseen Species):** In automated sorting lines, bins, or field crates, models trained exclusively on a small palette of target crops (e.g., apple, banana, and orange) frequently encounter non-target produce species (e.g., mango, guava, jujube, pomegranate, or lime). Under standard closed-set inference, deep networks lack an explicit rejection mechanism; they are forced to assign an unseen species to one of the $K$ known classes, often with uncalibrated, erroneously high confidence [8], [9].
3. **Treatment-Labelled Condition Shift:** In certain agricultural supply-chain and research contexts, produce may be subjected to chemical preservative treatments or immersion protocols intended to retard visible senescence. In curated benchmarks such as FruitVision [12], images are documented under a specific dataset-reported post-harvest treatment protocol involving immersion in a 10% formalin and 90% water solution for 30 minutes, followed by drying and photography 48 hours later. Whether visual models trained on natural produce can reliably distinguish images recorded under such post-harvest treatment protocols remains an empirical question.

Under standard softmax classification, neural networks are notoriously overconfident [8]. Logit outputs normalized via the softmax function produce probability distributions that often peak sharply near 1.0 even when the input image is heavily corrupted, semantically anomalous, or completely out-of-distribution [8], [9]. In automated quality sorting, an overconfident false-positive classification—such as misclassifying an unseen non-target species or an anomalous produce lot as a fresh known-fruit class—presents substantial commercial and quality-assurance liabilities.

### 1.3. Open-Set Recognition and Out-of-Distribution Detection Foundations
To address closed-set failure modes, computer vision research has developed two closely related paradigms: Open Set Recognition (OSR) [13], [14] and Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Detection [9], [15]. 

The concept of open-set recognition was formalized by Scheirer et al. [13], who defined the objective as simultaneously classifying known training classes while bounding the open space risk—the risk of incorrectly labeling an unknown, novel class sample as any known class. Bendale and Boult [14] extended this concept to deep neural networks through OpenMax, which estimates the probability of an input belonging to an unknown class by fitting Weibull distributions to activation vectors in the penultimate layer.

Simultaneously, the broader machine learning community formalized generalized out-of-distribution detection [9], [15]. Hendrycks and Gimpel [9] established the Maximum Softmax Probability (MSP) baseline, showing that correctly classified in-distribution samples typically exhibit higher maximum posterior probabilities than anomalous inputs. Liang et al. [16] developed ODIN, which amplifies the separation between in-distribution and out-of-distribution samples using temperature-scaled softmax outputs and input-space gradient perturbations. Lee et al. [17] introduced a feature-space Mahalanobis distance metric that models class-conditional Gaussian distributions across intermediate hidden representations. More recently, Liu et al. [18] proposed Energy-based OOD detection, utilizing the log-sum-exp of the classifier logits to circumvent the probability saturation that characterizes standard softmax normalization [15], [18]. Additionally, Vaze et al. [19] demonstrated that closed-set classifier capacity and representation quality strongly dictate open-set discrimination performance.

In operational deployment, OOD detectors are typically implemented either as continuous ranking functions or as discrete rejection gates governed by a pre-calibrated threshold $\tau$. When an input sample produces an anomaly score that violates the threshold, the system abstains from classification, triggering manual inspection or secondary non-optical assay.

### 1.4. Prior Agricultural OSR/OOD Studies and Specific Research Gaps
Open-set recognition and anomaly detection have recently attracted growing attention in agricultural vision. Several recent investigations have examined related challenges:
- Sattar et al. [20] developed SensorNet, combining computer vision, deep learning, and chemical sensors to identify chemical adulteration in fruit specimens.
- Bijoy et al. [12] introduced FruitVision, an image dataset containing fresh, rotten, and formalin-mixed fruit images.
- Wang et al. [21] proposed lightweight attention networks with unsupervised domain adaptation for cross-domain fruit classification.
- Pehlivan and Kara [22] introduced a lightweight one-shot open-set metric learning framework for food recognition in smart appliances.
- Jansi and Kavitha [23] developed PEOS-Net, a prototype-energy open-set network for robust crop leaf disease recognition beyond the closed-world assumption.
- Yan et al. [24] formulated PAG-MSBNet, using prototype-aware gated multi-scale branch networks for open-set crop disease recognition.

While these studies highlight the importance of non-standard conditions in agricultural classification, existing research has not systematically evaluated how established post-hoc OOD scoring functions behave when a frozen classifier faces multi-source external distributions. Specifically, four key empirical gaps remain:

1. **Gap 1: Limited Evaluation Beyond Dataset-Specific Closed-Set Splits:** Existing fruit-freshness studies predominantly report supervised classification performance on random train/test partitions within a single, curated dataset [4]–[6]. Such closed-set protocols evaluate neither model behavior on external image repositories nor discrimination against out-of-distribution instances. Furthermore, recent evaluations of agricultural image collections indicate that random partitioning within single repositories can allow near-duplicate images across splits, yielding optimistic closed-set performance estimates that do not reflect external test conditions [10], [11].
2. **Gap 2: Cross-Repository Confounding in OOD Benchmarks:** In open-set evaluations where out-of-distribution cohorts are drawn from a different repository than the known-class training data, differences in camera hardware, ambient illumination, and background context present a substantial methodological risk. Under such unstratified designs, repository-level acquisition artifacts can be confounded with true semantic divergence between fruit species [15]. Evaluating known and unknown categories collected under shared repository conditions (source matching) reduces repository-level confounding, though potential within-repository capture variations may remain.
3. **Gap 3: Empirical Behavior Under Treatment-Labelled Shifts:** While multimodal sensing has explored chemical adulteration detection combining optical imaging with specialized gas sensors [20], the behavior of post-hoc OOD scoring methods on a standard frozen RGB classifier facing dataset-reported treatment conditions [12] without auxiliary chemical sensors warrants systematic empirical investigation.
4. **Gap 4: Distinguishing Retrospective Ranking from Operational Threshold Calibration:** Many OOD detection studies primarily report retrospective metrics: AUROC assesses ranking discrimination across all decision thresholds without selecting an operating point, whereas FPR@95 evaluates the false positive rate at a retrospective threshold chosen post-hoc to achieve 95% true positive rate on the test cohort [9], [15], [18]. While suitable for relative algorithm comparison, retrospective metrics do not reflect operational deployment, where a discrete acceptance threshold must be pre-calibrated on validation data and held fixed during external evaluation. Measuring operational Unknown False Acceptance Rates (UFAR) alongside Known-Class Rejection Rates (KRR) under fixed validation thresholds provides complementary, deployment-context measurements of operational trade-offs.

### 1.5. Research Objectives and Formal Research Questions
This investigation addresses these gaps by conducting an extensive, source-stratified empirical evaluation of out-of-distribution detection in fruit image classification. The study evaluates a frozen MobileNetV2 six-class visual classifier across $N = 13,465$ image records curated from three distinct repositories under the Stage 4.0.3 external evaluation protocol. 

The paper specifically investigates three core research questions:
- **Research Question 1 (RQ1 — External Generalization):** How does the closed-set classification performance (top-1 accuracy, macro-F1, and balanced accuracy) of a lightweight MobileNetV2 model change when evaluated on external repositories containing the same known fruit classes?
- **Research Question 2 (RQ2 — OOD Discrimination):** How effectively do established OOD scoring mechanisms (MSP, Energy, ODIN, Mahalanobis distance, OpenMax) discriminate between in-distribution produce and distribution shifts when evaluated in source-matched cohorts examining (a) unseen-species semantic shift, (b) treatment-labelled condition shift, and (c) combined species-and-treatment shifts?
- **Research Question 3 (RQ3 — Operational Reliability):** How do fixed validation-calibrated rejection thresholds ($\tau_{95}$) affect out-of-distribution false acceptance rates (UFAR) and known-class rejection rates (KRR) under external domain and condition shifts? (Where known-class retention is defined as $1 - \text{KRR}$.)

### 1.6. Evidence-Supported Research Contributions
The primary contribution of this work is an empirical source-stratified evaluation, rigorous uncertainty audit, and diagnostic failure analysis. The study does not propose a novel standalone OOD algorithm, nor does it claim real-world agricultural supply chain deployment. The four specific, evidence-backed contributions of this study are:

1. **Audited Source-Stratified Benchmark Census ($N = 13,465$):** We establish an audited image-level evaluation benchmark comprising 13,465 image records structured across five distinct partitions: Partition A (Primary ID Test, $N=227$), Partition B (External Known-Class Shift, $N=5,350$), Partition C (Treatment-Labelled Condition Shift, $N=1,917$), Partition D1 (Unseen-Species Shift, $N=4,776$), and Partition D2 (Combined Shift, $N=1,195$). We incorporate exact-duplicate handling and perceptual-hash (pHash) sensitivity analysis across Hamming distance radii $r \in \{0, 4, 8\}$.
2. **Quantification of Closed-Set Performance Differences Across Partitions:** We evaluate the frozen MobileNetV2 classifier across partitions, observing a 28.56 percentage-point difference in top-1 accuracy between the primary in-distribution test partition (Partition A: 96.92% [94.71%, 98.68%]) and the pooled external known-class partition (Partition B: 68.36% [67.20%, 69.40%]), with balanced accuracy decreasing from 97.09% to 68.66%. Through source stratification, we show that performance varies across external repositories (FruitVision B: 69.14% accuracy, 69.56% balanced accuracy; Sultana B: 65.63% accuracy, 65.55% balanced accuracy), with closed-set errors heavily concentrated in the fresh apple class (where top-1 accuracy drops to 32.12% across pooled external images due to confusion with rotten apple and rotten orange).
3. **Source-Matched Evaluation Across Semantic and Treatment Cohorts:** By benchmarking within shared repositories, source matching reduces cross-repository confounding while potential within-repository capture variation remains. For the Within-FruitVision known-versus-unseen-species comparison (Cohort A), Energy-based detection achieves an AUROC of 81.57% [80.81%, 82.38%] with an operational UFAR of 13.18% [12.07%, 14.34%] ($366/2,776$ image records at $\tau_{\text{Energy}}=1.054579$), whereas discrimination was lower on the Within-Sultana comparison (Cohort D: Energy AUROC of 67.52% [65.67%, 69.25%], operational UFAR of 34.15% [32.35%, 35.90%], $683/2,000$ image records).
4. **Empirical Evaluation of Treatment-Labelled Images and Operational Trade-Offs:** In the evaluated FruitVision treatment-labelled cohort (Cohort B), the frozen classifier and tested post-hoc OOD scoring methods did not reliably distinguish images labelled under the dataset-reported treatment condition from the natural known-class cohort, with Energy achieving an AUROC of 53.13% [52.16%, 54.09%]. Under locked validation operating thresholds ($\tau_{95}$), the Dual-Gate cascade yields an operational UFAR-Fresh of 42.83% [41.26%, 44.34%] ($821/1,917$ image records), accepting and assigning a Fresh class to these images. Furthermore, for the Dual-Gate cascade under $\tau_{95}$, an operational UFAR of 16.07% [14.77%, 17.40%] ($446/2,776$ image records) on FruitVision unseen species incurs an external known-class rejection rate (KRR) of 41.98% [40.49%, 43.49%] ($1,745/4,157$ image records, corresponding to a retention rate of $1 - \text{KRR} \approx 58.02\%$) on FruitVision Known, and 42.43% [41.10%, 43.76%] ($2,270/5,350$ image records) across pooled external known produce (Partition B).
