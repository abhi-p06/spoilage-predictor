\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

This section reviews the scientific literature foundational to the present study across four key domains: (1) computer vision methodologies for fruit quality assessment and freshness classification; (2) cross-domain generalization and domain adaptation in agricultural vision systems; (3) established open-set recognition and out-of-distribution (OOD) detection algorithms; and (4) recent applications of open-set and anomaly detection frameworks to food and agricultural imagery. The section concludes with a structured comparison matrix and an explicit definition of the research gap addressed by this study.

\subsection{Computer Vision for Fruit Quality Classification}
\label{sec:cv_fruit_quality}

Automated post-harvest quality assessment has progressed significantly over the past two decades, transitioning from classical hand-crafted optical feature extraction to deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) \cite{rizzo2023fruit}. Early automated sorting systems relied predominantly on engineered representations of colour, morphological geometry, and surface texture \cite{rizzo2023fruit}. Colour histograms computed across RGB, HSV, and CIE $L^*a^*b^*$ spaces served as primary indicators of commercial maturity, chlorophyll degradation, and surface pigmentation changes. Morphological descriptors---including planar area, eccentricity, circularity, and perimeter irregularity---were leveraged to identify mechanical malformations and physical grading deviations. Texture descriptors, such as Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrices (GLCM) and local binary patterns (LBP), provided statistical representations of surface coarseness, fungal lesions, and skin shrivelling. However, these classical pipelines exhibited acute vulnerability to illumination variations, background reflections, camera sensor discrepancies, and natural biological phenotypic diversity within identical cultivars \cite{rizzo2023fruit}.

The advent of deep convolutional architectures enabled end-to-end hierarchical representation learning, capturing subtle non-linear manifestations of produce senescence, mechanical bruising, and microbial decay without manual feature engineering \cite{rizzo2023fruit,zhang2024fruit}. Multi-task convolutional networks have demonstrated the ability to concurrently estimate fruit categories, ripeness stages, and defect severity levels from unified photographic inputs \cite{zhang2024fruit}. Specialized deep architectures have achieved high classification accuracy on curated benchmarks for distinguishing fresh from rotten produce across common commercial species \cite{chen2024classification,gulzar2023fruit}.

In post-harvest logistics, packing facilities, and farm-gate sorting stations, computational efficiency and hardware constraints represent primary deployment criteria. High-throughput sorting conveyors and handheld mobile grading devices cannot accommodate computationally intensive, high-parameter vision backbones. Consequently, lightweight architectures have become the standard for embedded agricultural vision \cite{chen2024classification}. In particular, the MobileNetV2 architecture \cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2} has been widely adopted due to its use of depthwise separable convolutions, inverted residual blocks, and linear bottleneck layers. With approximately $2.26\times 10^6$ trainable parameters (at standard width multiplier $\alpha=1.0$), MobileNetV2 dramatically curtails floating-point operations while preserving high discriminative capacity. Deep transfer learning---wherein backbones pre-trained on large-scale natural image datasets (such as ImageNet) are fine-tuned on target agricultural domains---has further enabled robust feature extraction even when localized training sets are constrained in sample size \cite{gulzar2023fruit,chen2024classification}.

Despite high reported accuracies under closed-set evaluation protocols, photographic image acquisition in practical agricultural environments introduces severe distributional challenges. Ambient lighting fluctuations, direct sunlight glint, variable artificial warehouse illumination, diverse photographic backgrounds, and camera sensor colour-balance differences introduce pervasive domain shift \cite{abayomi2024fruitq,sultana2022extensive}. The FruitQ repository introduced by Abayomi-Alli et al. \cite{abayomi2024fruitq} provided a structured multi-fruit dataset comprising 11 fruit varieties categorized into three freshness states (Fresh, Mild, and Rotten), evaluating five baseline deep learning architectures (ShuffleNet, SqueezeNet, EfficientNet, ResNet18, and MobileNetV2) under closed-set protocols.

The empirical evaluation conducted in the present study examines photographic produce records across multiple repositories, drawing upon two primary external dataset publications:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Sultana et al. (2022)} \cite{sultana2022extensive}: Introduced a photographic dataset for recognizing fresh and rotten fruit specimens (\textit{Data in Brief}, 44:108552). The repository encompasses 16 fresh and rotten categories representing eight fruit types: grape, guava, jujube, pomegranate, strawberry, apple, banana, and orange. Images were collected from retail fruit shops and authentic agricultural field environments under natural ambient lighting, with collection and condition labelling conducted with assistance from an agricultural domain specialist.
\item \textbf{Bijoy et al. (2025)} \cite{bijoy2025fruitvision}: Introduced FruitVision, a benchmark dataset for fresh, rotten, and formalin-mixed fruit detection (\textit{Data in Brief}, 61:111752). The dataset comprises five fruit species (apple, banana, mango, orange, and grape) evaluated across three dataset-reported visual conditions: fresh, rotten, and a treatment-labelled condition (immersion in a 10\% formalin and 90\% water solution for 30 minutes, followed by 48-hour drying and photography, as documented by the dataset authors). The present study evaluates these photographic records under their dataset-reported labels and does not conduct chemical assays or independently verify formalin residue. Image collection was conducted using three distinct mobile smartphone cameras across varied indoor and outdoor lighting environments. The published raw collection comprises 10,154 original photographs, with geometric and photometric augmentations expanding the published archive to 81,232 images.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent \textit{Evaluation Census Clarification:} We emphasize that the published raw and augmented archive sizes reported by dataset authors (e.g., the 10,154 raw or 81,232 augmented images in FruitVision) represent upstream repository totals. These figures must not be confused with the audited benchmark cohort evaluated in the present study. As detailed in Section~\ref{sec:dataset_design}, our evaluation utilizes an audited local census of exactly 13,465 evaluated image records (Primary Kaggle: 227; FruitVision: 10,045; Sultana: 3,193) across five dedicated partitions. Every evaluated image record is uniquely tracked through cryptographic hashes, exact duplicates are systematically audited, and perceptual near-duplicates are evaluated via sensitivity analysis across Hamming distance thresholds. We explicitly note that image records cannot be assumed to represent independent biological specimens.

\subsection{Cross-Domain and External Generalization in Fruit Classification}
\label{sec:cross_domain_generalization}

Supervised machine learning algorithms are traditionally grounded in the closed-set, independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) assumption, wherein training and test samples are drawn from an identical underlying probability distribution: $P_{\text{train}}(X, Y) = P_{\text{test}}(X, Y)$ \cite{yang2024generalized}. In practical agricultural deployments, however, this assumption rarely holds due to diverse distributional shifts. To analyze these phenomena rigorously, we maintain precise distinctions among shift mechanisms \cite{yang2024generalized}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Covariate Shift:} The marginal input feature distribution varies between source and target domains ($P_s(X) \ne P_t(X)$) while the posterior class mapping remains invariant ($P_s(Y \mid X) = P_t(Y \mid X)$). In agricultural imaging, covariate shift arises from variations in camera sensor optics, ambient illumination, background surfaces, capture geometry, and regional cultivar appearance differences.
\item \textbf{Label Shift:} The prior class distribution shifts between domains ($P_s(Y) \ne P_t(Y)$) while the class-conditional input densities $P(X \mid Y)$ remain identical.
\item \textbf{Concept Shift:} The conditional relationship between visual features and semantic class labels changes between environments ($P_s(Y \mid X) \ne P_t(Y \mid X)$), such that identical visual appearances correspond to different quality states.
\item \textbf{Open-Set Semantic Shift:} The target domain contains semantic classes whose support lies outside the source taxonomy: $\operatorname{supp}(P_t(Y)) \not\subseteq \operatorname{supp}(P_s(Y))$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent In this taxonomy, the FruitVision treatment-labelled cohort represents images of known fruit species carrying a dataset-reported formalin-mixed condition. We do not classify this condition as concept shift or a new semantic class, nor do we conflate post-harvest treatment-labelled shifts with label-space expansion. Rather, it represents an empirical condition shift wherein visual manifestations of post-harvest immersion are assessed against a classifier trained on natural produce, without implying independent chemical residue detection.

In agricultural image analysis, it is also essential to distinguish between \textit{domain adaptation} and \textit{cross-domain evaluation without adaptation}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Domain Adaptation:} Methods in this paradigm actively adjust model parameters, feature representations, or latent feature alignments between a known source domain $\mathcal{D}_s$ and a target domain $\mathcal{D}_t$ during training or test-time calibration. Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) utilizes unlabelled target-domain images to minimize divergence measures (such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy or Correlation Alignment) or employs adversarial domain discriminators to project source and target features into a domain-invariant latent space.
\item \textbf{Cross-Domain Evaluation Without Adaptation:} Cross-domain evaluation without adaptation provides a diagnostic assessment of model performance on selected external repositories. It does not, by itself, establish performance under physical field deployment. Instead, this protocol evaluates the direct, unadapted generalization of a pre-trained classifier when exposed to external photographic datasets without access to target-domain training data, fine-tuning steps, or parameter updates, benchmarking baseline failure modes when adaptation is unavailable.
\end{itemize}

A notable contribution in agricultural domain adaptation is the study by Wang et al. (2023) \cite{wang2023crossdomain}, titled \textit{``A cross-domain fruit classification method based on lightweight attention networks and unsupervised domain adaptation''} (\textit{Complex \& Intelligent Systems}, 9(4):4227--4247). Wang et al. proposed HAM-MobileNet, integrating a hybrid spatial-channel attention module into MobileNetV3, coupled with an unsupervised domain adaptation framework that minimizes a domain discrepancy loss combining Local Maximum Mean Discrepancy (LMMD) and Correlation Alignment (CORAL). Their framework was evaluated across two fruit classification benchmarks: (1) a grape classification dataset containing seven grape varieties across four domains (controlled environment, illumination variation, background variation, and internet images); and (2) a general fruit classification dataset containing 11 fruit types across two domains. Their findings demonstrated that active domain alignment significantly improves target-domain classification performance relative to unadapted baselines.

While Wang et al. \cite{wang2023crossdomain} provide valuable insights into active domain alignment, their methodology differs fundamentally from the present study. Wang et al. focus on model training and domain adaptation to maximize transfer performance. In contrast, the present investigation evaluates the diagnostic failure modes of a common \textit{frozen} MobileNetV2 classifier without adapting its weights to the target repositories. Rather than modifying model parameters, our study investigates: (1) the magnitude of external known-class accuracy differences across distinct repository sources; and (2) whether post-hoc out-of-distribution detection methods can reliably identify and reject out-of-domain samples before misclassification occurs. Furthermore, as the underlying datasets, species definitions, backbones, and evaluation protocols differ, reported numerical accuracy values cannot be compared directly across incompatible study protocols.

\subsection{Open-Set Recognition and Out-of-Distribution Detection}
\label{sec:osr_ood_detection}

When deployed in unconstrained real-world environments, computer vision models inevitably encounter visual inputs that deviate from their training distributions. The inability of standard closed-set classifiers to handle such inputs motivated the formalization of Open-Set Recognition (OSR) and generalized Out-of-Distribution (OOD) detection \cite{scheirer2013toward,bendale2016towards,yang2024generalized}.

Scheirer et al. (2013) \cite{scheirer2013toward} formalized open-set recognition as balancing empirical risk minimization on known training classes against open-space risk---the risk of erroneously assigning unknown inputs to known semantic classes. Bendale and Boult (2016) \cite{bendale2016towards} extended this principle to deep neural networks, demonstrating that deep classifiers suffer from intrinsic overconfidence: deep networks map inputs into linear feature spaces capped by a softmax activation function $\sigma(z)_k = \exp(z_k) / \sum_j \exp(z_j)$, which forces posterior probabilities to sum to unity. Consequently, an unfamiliar, anomalous, or unseen-class input can produce near-maximal softmax confidence if its projection aligns even slightly closer to one class weight vector than others, even when the input resides far from all known training clusters in latent space \cite{guo2017calibration,hendrycks2017baseline,vaze2022open}.

Generalized out-of-distribution detection expands open-set recognition beyond semantic class novelty, addressing covariate domain shifts, environmental background variations, and corrupted sensor inputs \cite{yang2024generalized}. In this study, we systematically evaluate five established post-hoc OOD scoring algorithms, applied to a common frozen MobileNetV2 classifier without architectural alteration:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Maximum Softmax Probability (MSP)} \cite{hendrycks2017baseline}: Formulated by Hendrycks and Gimpel (2017) as an established confidence baseline, MSP utilizes the maximum predicted posterior probability as the confidence score:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{MSP}}(x) = \max_{k \in \{1,\dots,K\}} \frac{\exp(z_k(x))}{\sum_{j=1}^K \exp(z_j(x))}
\label{eq:msp}
\end{equation}
where $z_k(x)$ represents the logit for class $k$. While computationally straightforward, MSP represents maximum softmax confidence scoring rather than calibrated probability estimation, and it frequently fails under severe distribution shift due to softmax probability saturation on out-of-distribution inputs.

\item \textbf{OpenMax} \cite{bendale2016towards}: Introduced by Bendale and Boult (2016), OpenMax replaces the standard softmax layer by modeling the distribution of penultimate-layer activation vectors for each known class using Extreme Value Theory (EVT). Class-conditional mean activation vectors (MAVs) are computed on training data, and Weibull distributions are fitted to the largest distances between training samples and their respective MAVs. For an operational input $x$, OpenMax computes class-specific rejection probabilities, modifies activation magnitudes, and redistributes activation mass to an explicit ``unknown'' class ($P_0(x)$). An input is accepted only if $1 - P_0(x)$ exceeds a pre-calibrated threshold.

\item \textbf{ODIN (Out-of-Distribution detector for Neural networks)} \cite{liang2018enhancing}: Liang et al. (2018) demonstrated that OOD detection can be significantly enhanced without retraining through two post-hoc operations: temperature scaling ($T > 1$) and input-space gradient perturbation. Logits are scaled by temperature $T$, smoothing the probability distribution, while the input image $x$ is perturbed via gradient ascent:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{x} = x - \epsilon \operatorname{sign}\left(-\nabla_x \log \frac{\exp(z_{\hat{y}}(x)/T)}{\sum_{j=1}^K \exp(z_j(x)/T)}\right)
\label{eq:odin}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{y} = \arg\max_k z_k(x)$. This directional perturbation increases the maximum softmax probability of in-distribution instances substantially more than that of out-of-distribution instances, amplifying the separation margin.

\item \textbf{Mahalanobis Feature Distance} \cite{lee2018simple}: Lee et al. (2018) proposed modeling penultimate feature representations $f(x)$ as a mixture of class-conditional multivariate Gaussians with class centroids $\mu_k$ and a tied covariance matrix $\mathbf{\Sigma} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{i: y_i=k} (f(x_i) - \mu_k)(f(x_i) - \mu_k)^\top$. The anomaly score is defined as the negative minimum Mahalanobis distance to the nearest class centroid:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{Maha}}(x) = -\min_{k \in \{1,\dots,K\}} (f(x) - \mu_k)^\top \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} (f(x) - \mu_k).
\label{eq:mahalanobis}
\end{equation}
This squared distance quantifies the statistical deviation of the latent feature representation under the fitted class-conditional Gaussian feature model. In empirical implementations, the negated Euclidean Mahalanobis distance $-\sqrt{(f(x) - \mu_k)^\top \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} (f(x) - \mu_k)}$ is frequently computed instead; because the square root is a strictly monotonic transformation on non-negative quadratic distances, it preserves sample ranking, AUROC, and directional orientation without altering thresholded decision properties.

\item \textbf{Energy-Based Out-of-Distribution Detection} \cite{liu2020energy}: Liu et al. (2020) demonstrated that mapping logits directly to Helmholtz free energy provides a theoretically grounded OOD scoring function that avoids the normalisation artifacts of softmax probabilities. The negative free energy is defined as:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{Energy}}(x) = -E(x; f) = T \cdot \log \sum_{k=1}^K \exp\left(\frac{z_k(x)}{T}\right)
\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
The energy score is derived from the log-sum-exp of the classifier logits and is motivated by an energy-based interpretation of the classifier \cite{liu2020energy}. In this study, it is used as an OOD ranking score, not as a calibrated estimate of the true input marginal density.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent \textit{Methodological Positioning:} These five algorithms represent distinct mathematical principles: maximum softmax confidence scoring (MSP), extreme value activation modeling (OpenMax), temperature and input gradient perturbation (ODIN), multivariate Gaussian feature distance (Mahalanobis), and unnormalized logit thermodynamics (Energy). None of these algorithms was specifically engineered for agricultural imagery; they were designed and benchmarked on general vision tasks (CIFAR, SVHN, ImageNet) \cite{yang2024generalized}. In this study, we apply these established post-hoc scoring functions to a common frozen MobileNetV2 produce classifier across source-stratified fruit cohorts. Furthermore, the operational Energy--MSP Dual-Gate cascade investigated in our operational analysis is an empirical decision policy combining Energy and MSP at validation order-statistic operating thresholds ($\tau_{95}$), and does not represent a new theoretical OOD scoring algorithm.

\subsection{Open-Set Recognition in Food and Agricultural Applications}
\label{sec:agri_osr_applications}

Recognizing the vulnerability of closed-set classifiers in unconstrained operational settings, recent studies have begun exploring open-set recognition and anomaly detection within agricultural and food-processing domains.

\textbf{Sattar et al. (2024) --- SensorNet} \cite{sattar2024sensornet}: Titled \textit{``A comprehensive approach to detecting chemical adulteration in fruits using computer vision, deep learning, and chemical sensors''} (\textit{Intelligent Systems with Applications}, 23:200402), this study investigated the detection of chemical adulteration (specifically formaldehyde immersion) in fruits. Sattar et al. formulated SensorNet, a multimodal framework integrating captured fruit imagery with chemical-sensor measurements from an electronic sensor array sensitive to formaldehyde gas. An essential methodological distinction exists between SensorNet and the present investigation: SensorNet is a multimodal sensor-fusion system that integrates optical images with direct chemical-sensor measurements. In contrast, our study evaluates image-level classification on photographic records carrying dataset-reported chemical treatment labels, with no independent chemical measurements conducted in the present benchmark. Because the sensing modalities and target outputs differ fundamentally, numerical classification accuracy in SensorNet cannot be directly compared to optical OOD AUROC or operational false acceptance rates.

\textbf{Pehlivan and Kara (2026)} \cite{pehlivan2026lightweight}: Titled \textit{``A Lightweight One-Shot Open-Set Metric Learning Framework for Food Recognition and Decision Support in Smart Ovens''} (\textit{Electronics}, 15(16):3533), this study developed an open-set metric learning framework using a modified MobileNetV2 architecture for food recognition and unknown object rejection in smart kitchen appliances. By replacing the traditional softmax layer with normalized feature embeddings evaluated via cosine similarity against stored class centroids, their system rejects unknown food items below a calibrated similarity threshold. While Pehlivan and Kara utilize a MobileNetV2 backbone, their application domain focuses on one-shot recognition of prepared food dishes within automated domestic cooking cavities. In contrast, our study evaluates whole biological produce across multi-source external photographic repositories under natural post-harvest distribution shifts.

\textbf{Jansi and Kavitha --- PEOS-Net (2026)} \cite{jansi2026peos}: Titled \textit{``PEOS-Net: A Prototype-Energy Open-Set Network for Robust Tomato Leaf Disease Recognition Beyond the Closed-World Assumption''} (\textit{Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence}, Volume 9, Article 1938398; published October 30, 2026), this peer-reviewed study introduced a prototype-energy open-set network for crop leaf disease recognition using a frozen EfficientNet-B0 backbone. The architecture couples a prototype-based classification branch with an energy-based OOD scoring branch, evaluated on an eight-class tomato subset of the PlantVillage repository, where two tomato disease classes were held out as unknown categories. The study incorporates held-out calibration and test partitions, multiple random seeds, and statistical evaluations. An important limitation of the study is that the unknown classes were drawn from the identical PlantVillage repository, crop species, and imaging environment as the known training classes. Consequently, PEOS-Net represents a within-dataset class-holdout evaluation and does not establish external field-condition robustness. Furthermore, because foliar pathology involves localized planar lesions on leaves rather than whole-fruit volumetric senescence, its task setting differs fundamentally from post-harvest produce grading.

\textbf{Yan et al. --- PAG-MSBNet (2026)} \cite{yan2026pagmsbnet}: Titled \textit{``PAG-MSBNet: Prototype-aware gated multi-scale branch network for open-set crop disease recognition''} (\textit{Ecological Informatics}, Volume 95, Article 103703, 2026), this study formulated a multi-scale spatial-channel branch network incorporating prototype-aware gating mechanisms to classify known crop diseases while rejecting novel pathologies across public plant disease benchmarks, including PlantVillage and Paddy Doctor. Yan et al. focus on custom architectural engineering for open-set crop leaf diagnostics. In contrast, our study evaluates a standard, frozen commercial vision backbone (MobileNetV2) across external photographic repositories of harvested fruits, quantifying empirical failure modes under source-stratified covariate, species, and post-harvest treatment shifts without modifying the underlying model architecture.

\subsubsection{Critical Synthesis and Specific Research Gap}
\label{sec:research_gap}

The studies reviewed address complementary problems in fruit-quality classification, domain adaptation, chemical-sensor-assisted adulteration detection, and open-set recognition. The present study examines a particular combination of evaluation dimensions: established post-hoc OOD scores applied to a common frozen fruit-quality classifier, evaluated across external known-class, unseen-species, treatment-labelled, and combined-shift cohorts, with source-matched comparisons and fixed validation-calibrated operational thresholds.

Specifically, the present study investigates four evaluation dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Supervised Closed-Set Classification vs.\ External OOD Evaluation:} Existing fruit-freshness studies predominantly report supervised classification performance under random train/test partitions within single curated datasets \cite{zhang2024fruit,chen2024classification,gulzar2023fruit,abayomi2024fruitq}. Such evaluations do not assess how classifiers behave when exposed to external repositories or out-of-distribution instances. Our study addresses this gap by assessing a frozen classifier on external multi-repository cohorts and evaluating post-hoc OOD detection.
\item \textbf{Source-Matched Cohort Comparisons to Mitigate Cross-Repository Confounding:} In open-set evaluations where out-of-distribution cohorts are drawn from an external repository distinct from the training set, differences in camera hardware, ambient illumination, and background context present a substantial methodological risk of being confounded with true semantic divergence \cite{yang2024generalized}. Source-matched comparisons are conducted within the same repository to reduce repository-level confounding, though potential within-repository capture variations may remain.
\item \textbf{Empirical Evaluation of Treatment-Labelled Images:} While multimodal systems (such as SensorNet \cite{sattar2024sensornet}) incorporate specialized chemical gas sensors to identify adulteration, our study evaluates whether standard RGB imagery contains sufficient optical separation signal for post-hoc OOD scoring methods applied to a standard frozen classifier to distinguish treatment-labelled images (documented under a dataset-reported 10\% formalin immersion protocol \cite{bijoy2025fruitvision}) from natural produce, without claiming independent chemical detection.
\item \textbf{Distinguishing Retrospective Ranking from Operational Threshold Calibration:} Many OOD detection studies prioritize retrospective metrics: AUROC assesses ranking discrimination across all possible decision thresholds without selecting an operating point, whereas FPR@95 evaluates the false positive rate at a retrospective threshold chosen post-hoc to achieve 95\% true positive rate on the test cohort \cite{hendrycks2017baseline,yang2024generalized,liu2020energy}. While suitable for relative algorithm comparison, retrospective metrics do not reflect operational deployment, where a discrete acceptance threshold must be pre-calibrated on validation data and held fixed during external evaluation. Measuring operational Unknown False Acceptance Rates (UFAR) alongside Known-Class Rejection Rates (KRR) under fixed validation thresholds provides complementary, deployment-context measurements of operational trade-offs, supported by 1,000-iteration non-parametric bootstrap confidence intervals and perceptual-hash (pHash) near-duplicate sensitivity analysis across an audited $13,465$-record image census.
\end{enumerate}
